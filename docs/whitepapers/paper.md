## Abstract

Attention normalizes each query's compatibilities within its own context, an idea older than the transformer (kernel regression, fast weights). Its first widely adopted form in computational biology, context likelihood of relatedness (CLR), remains standard for de novo, unsupervised network inference but stops at the scores. We bring back from language modelling a temperature-controlled softmax, iterated attention and depth chosen on held-out data without labels. On strong-evidence *Escherichia coli* regulons, attention raises pooled recovery 16% over CLR, in all 1,000 resamplings, and, controlling for hub genes, leads GENIE3 and PIDC; the gain depends on CLR's normalization. Per regulon, it ties CLR and GENIE3. Iterated attention trades pair-level resolution for neighbourhood aggregation, helping large, loose regulons and hurting tight co-expression cliques such as ribosomal complexes. Gains are small in human K562 cells, and on single cells attention adds nothing to rank-based CLR. [PENDING] Zero-shot attention from single-cell foundation models [falls short of] both.

## Introduction

Attention is now the dominant way in which machine-learning models relate the elements of a set.  For each query, a compatibility with every key is computed, normalized across keys (usually by a softmax with a temperature) and used to aggregate values; stacking the operation lets information travel along multi-step paths.  In transformers the compatibilities are dot products of learned embeddings, and what to attend to is learned from large corpora (Bahdanau et al. 2014; Vaswani et al. 2017).

The pattern is older than the transformer, and we do not claim that it began in biology.  Kernel regression weights observations by their normalized similarity to a query and has been a standard statistical tool since the 1960s (Nadaraya 1964; Watson 1964), and fast-weight programmers are formally equivalent to linear attention (Schmidhuber 1991; Schlag et al. 2021).  Our claim is narrower:  in computational biology, attention-like normalization entered everyday practice independently, a decade before the transformer, through a method for inferring gene regulatory networks.

Context likelihood of relatedness (CLR; Faith, Hayete et al. 2007) estimates the mutual information between every pair of genes in an expression compendium and expresses each value as a z-score against the distribution of that gene's dependences on all other genes.  Read as an operator, each gene is a query, every other gene a key, and the compatibility an estimated statistical dependence rather than a learned dot product, normalized per query against the query's own context.  That normalization is what made CLR work:  a gene weakly dependent on everything is not a hub, whereas a modest dependence that stands out from a gene's own background is a candidate regulatory link.  Topological overlap (Ravasz et al. 2002; Zhang & Horvath 2005), which predates CLR, also normalizes, but it normalizes counts of shared neighbours in an already thresholded network by the genes' connectivity; it does not calibrate each gene's raw dependences against that gene's own background.

CLR became a standard tool for de novo network inference, that is, inference from expression data alone.  Methods that bring informative priors, such as pathways, binding motifs or curated interactions, can outperform it, but they answer a different question.  Among expression-only methods, CLR has remained a usual unsupervised baseline for nearly two decades.  It was one of the 'well-established off-the-shelf' methods of the DREAM5 network-inference challenge and a component of several of its ensemble predictors (Marbach et al. 2012), and on a current single-cell benchmark it remains comparable to tree-ensemble regression and to partial information decomposition (Results).  It is not the top scorer everywhere:  in DREAM5, regression-based tree ensembles, which fit a predictive model for each gene from a supplied list of candidate regulators, scored higher on directed edges from transcription factors to targets.  CLR is different in kind.  It is model-free, unsupervised estimation of pairwise dependence with per-gene calibration, and it needs neither a regulator list nor a predictive model, so it applies equally when regulators are unknown or latent.  To our knowledge it is the first attention-like operator to be widely adopted in computational biology.

CLR, however, stopped at the scores.  It ranked them as edges and never used the rest of attention:  a temperature-controlled normalization, aggregation and depth.  Over the same two decades, attention research in natural-language processing turned these components into a mature toolkit.  Here we bring that toolkit back to the setting in which CLR has proved durable, and measure what each component adds and what it costs.  On the compendium on which CLR was validated, completing CLR as attention improves pooled recovery of bacterial regulons, robustly to resampling of experiments and to control for hub genes, and only when the attention acts on CLR's context-normalized scores.  Per regulon it is on par with CLR and GENIE3.  The walk trades pair-level resolution for neighbourhood aggregation:  it helps large, loose regulatory programs and hurts tight co-expression cliques and direct edges, so it is a module ranker, not a better inferrer of direct regulatory edges.

The comparison that matters most to machine learning is with learned attention.  Independent evaluations have found that attention and embeddings from single-cell foundation models add little beyond simple baselines (Boiarsky et al. 2024; Kedzierska et al. 2025; Ahlmann-Eltze et al. 2025; Kendiukhov 2026, the last on perturbation-target prediction rather than network recovery).  An estimated attention operator that needs no training data, embeddings or labels, and whose scores carry a permutation null, is a natural reference point for them.  We have preregistered a head-to-head comparison with zero-shot attention from scPRINT and scGPT on the same cells and truths [PENDING].

## Results

### CLR scores are attention scores, and completing them takes three components

Let D be a G × G matrix of pairwise dependences between genes estimated from an expression compendium (Fig. 1).  CLR computes for each row i the z-scores z_ij = (D_ij − μ_i)/σ_i, clips negative values to zero, and combines the two directions into a symmetric score S_ij = (z_ij + z_ji)/√2.  The row-wise standardization is a per-query normalization and plays the role that softmax plays in attention.

We add the three components that CLR lacked.  First, each row of S becomes attention weights through a softmax with a per-query temperature, A_i· = softmax(S_i·/τ_i), taken over the gene's 50 highest scores; τ_i is chosen so that the effective number of attended genes, exp(entropy), equals 10 for every gene, which makes attention comparably focused for genes whose score distributions differ widely.  Second, attention is iterated through the lazy operator P = ½(I + A), and the readout is the symmetric attention mass between genes after t steps, ½(Pᵗ + Pᵗᵀ).  Third, the depth t is selected without labels:  experiments are split by laboratory, the operator is built on one half, and t is chosen to maximize how well attention mass predicts the strongest CLR pairs of the other half.  No gold standard is used at any stage of model selection.

Iterated attention through a row-stochastic operator is a random walk, and the construction is therefore related to diffusion kernels (Kondor & Lafferty 2002; Zhou & Schölkopf 2004) and to network propagation (Cowen et al. 2017).  We treat this relationship as a baseline to beat rather than a novelty to claim, and every comparison below includes propagation on uncalibrated scores.

### Completed CLR attention improves pooled recovery of *E. coli* regulons

We evaluated the operator on the M3D *E. coli* compendium (4,297 genes; 907 microarrays from 466 experiments; Faith et al. 2008) against RegulonDB 14.5.  The benchmark is regulator-agnostic:  a gene pair is positive if both genes belong to the same regulon of any regulator class (transcription factors, small RNAs, sigma factors and ppGpp), and pairs within an operon are excluded.  At strong or confirmed evidence (SC) this gives 155 regulons, 1,879 genes and 1.76 million scored pairs with a positive rate of 9.4%.  We report pooled co-membership AUPR, which ranks all pairs together and is therefore dominated by large regulons, and per-regulon coherence, the AUROC of within-regulon pairs against pairs of a member and an unrelated gene.  Per-regulon average precision, which weights every regulon equally, is reported with the competitor comparison below.

On the 466 replicate-averaged experiments, one held-out laboratory split chose t* = 8 (the two directions gave 10 and 7).  At this depth attention reached a pooled AUPR of 0.157 against 0.136 for CLR, a difference of +0.021 (95% CI +0.015 to +0.027, paired delete-half jackknife over genes), and a median coherence of 0.632 against 0.597 (+0.031; +0.018 to +0.046, paired bootstrap over regulons).  Correlation and raw mutual information fell well below CLR (AUPR 0.116 and 0.115).  At all evidence levels the AUPR gain was smaller (+0.007 at this split), and the coherence gain that the regulon bootstrap found at this split did not survive resampling of experiments (next section).

Depth behaves as attention depth should (Fig. 2).  Pooled AUPR rises until about t = 27–32, whereas coherence peaks at t = 4–8 and collapses by t = 128, as deep propagation blurs specific programs into diffuse ones.  The label-free criterion selects the range in which both improve.  Over 50 random laboratory splits it chose a median t* of 9 (interquartile range 9–10, range 8–11; individual split directions 6–14), and the criterion lay within 1% of its maximum for t = 7–11, so the choice is stable and the optimum flat (Supplementary Note 4).  At t* = 9 the gains were +0.022 AUPR (+0.017 to +0.027) and +0.031 coherence (+0.019 to +0.043).  The held-out depth is conservative relative to the benchmark, whose pooled AUPR peaks near t = 27, so attention is not tuned to the gold standard.  The softmax candidate cap does not drive the result:  caps of 50, 100 and 200 give the same depth and gain (Supplementary Table 3).

### The gain survives resampling of experiments

Expression compendia contain technical replicates and clusters of related experiments, so resampling genes can understate uncertainty.  We therefore resampled experiments and recomputed dependences, CLR scores and attention from scratch in every draw.  In a cluster bootstrap that draws the 466 experiments with replacement and one replicate array per occurrence (1,000 draws), attention at t = 8 improved AUPR by +0.020 (2.5–97.5% interval +0.018 to +0.023) and coherence by +0.028 (+0.010 to +0.046) (Fig. 4).  A replicate bootstrap that removes pseudo-replication by using one array per experiment (200 draws) gave +0.021 (+0.020 to +0.022).  Because these intervals hold t fixed, we also ran a nested bootstrap in which every one of 1,000 cluster draws re-selects t on its own random laboratory split.  The selected depth varied little (median 10; 2.5–97.5% interval 8–12), and attention at each draw's own depth improved AUPR by +0.022 (+0.019 to +0.026), about 16% of CLR's 0.136, exceeding CLR in all 1,000 draws, and coherence by +0.026 (+0.008 to +0.045) (Supplementary Note 5).  At all evidence levels the AUPR gain remained significant (+0.009; +0.006 to +0.012) but the coherence gain did not (+0.011; −0.004 to +0.025).

Attention is also not a disguised correction for global expression programs.  After removing 0–20 principal components, or the number chosen by a permutation test, the gain over CLR remained significant at every level on both M3D (+0.017 to +0.022 AUPR) and EcoMAC (+0.020 to +0.029); removing two or three components improved CLR and attention alike (Supplementary Note 7).  The components removed are not noise.  In this compendium the first three principal components are the cell's dominant physiological programs:  growth rate (ribosomal-protein genes separate on the first component with an AUROC of 0.98), motility (the σ28 and FlhDC regulons on the second and third) and the stationary-phase response of σS.  Removing them sharpens the remaining regulons by deleting the condition-specific sigma-factor programs that dominate bacterial expression variance, which is why we keep them in the main analyses (Supplementary Note 8).

### Context normalization and the softmax drive the gain

To isolate each component we applied the same softmax to five score matrices, each at the depth selected by its own held-out criterion over 20 laboratory splits (Table 1; Supplementary Note 3).  Softmax propagation on uncalibrated |r| or mutual information recovered about a third of the AUPR gain and no significant coherence gain.  The full gain appeared only when attention acted on context-normalized scores, whichever dependence measure underlay them.

Each t* is self-consistent, chosen by how well an operator reproduces its own scores in held-out laboratories, so the depths are not tuned on a common yardstick.

The softmax matters as well as the normalization.  CLR scores carry a permutation null, so a natural alternative is hard selection:  keep the edges that pass a permutation false-discovery rate and propagate on that graph.  Hard selection alone leaves 62–77% of genes without neighbours, which cannot propagate.  Adding each gene's ten strongest neighbours makes it usable, and we compared such operators with soft attention inside the cluster bootstrap, on 200 draws paired with those of the main run (Supplementary Note 14).  The best hard operator (FDR 0.05 plus the top 10 neighbours) raised SC AUPR over CLR by +0.012 (+0.010 to +0.015) with no change in coherence and no gain on all-evidence regulons.  Soft attention beat it by 0.0075 AUPR (0.0053 to 0.0095), in all 200 paired draws, with 0.026 higher coherence.  The same hard top-10 propagation on correlation gave no gain in AUPR and lost coherence in the cluster bootstrap (−0.026; −0.046 to −0.005).

On this single-platform compendium the dependence measure mattered little:  CLR on correlation performed like CLR on mutual information, consistent with 99% of selected relationships being positive and monotone.  Heterogeneous data behave differently.  On EcoMAC (Carrera et al. 2014), which pools 2,198 arrays from two Affymetrix platforms and other sources, platform offsets bend pairwise relationships into composite shapes that mutual information registers and correlation largely misses.  Without batch correction CLR on mutual information gives higher regulon coherence than CLR on correlation, and it keeps a smaller advantage after per-platform correction; attention helps in every configuration (Supplementary Note 1).

### Shapley values rank the components

To summarize which components carry the gain, we switched six of them on and off in all 64 combinations and computed exact Shapley values for each metric (Fig. 3; `analysis/shapley.R`).  The six are the dependence measure, the binning rule, context normalization, the softmax, depth, and removal of two global principal components.  For co-membership AUPR, depth contributes most (+0.018), followed by context normalization (+0.013) and the removal of global components (+0.012).  For coherence, context normalization contributes most (+0.025), and depth contributes nothing on average.  Depth hurts coherence when it acts on uncalibrated scores and helps when it acts on CLR scores with a softmax, so its average contribution is near zero.  Correspondingly, removing the softmax from the full model costs the most coherence (−0.043).  Mutual information and the common binning rule contribute little on this single-platform compendium.  The all-evidence decomposition is in Supplementary Fig. 3.

### The lead is not an artefact of hub genes

A random walk converges toward well-connected genes, so deep attention could rank pairs by hubness rather than by shared regulation.  The stationary distribution π of the attention walk is indeed very skewed (maximum 310 times the median), and hubness alone carries real signal:  ranking pairs by π_i + π_j gives an AUROC of 0.546 on M3D SC regulons.  Dividing attention mass by π_i + π_j is therefore the wrong control, because it removes that signal from every method and strips CLR itself to 1.05 times the base rate.  We instead score every method by its within-stratum percentile in ten strata of π_i + π_j, so that methods are compared only on pairs of similar hubness (Methods; Supplementary Note 9).

Under this control attention stays ahead of CLR on M3D SC regulons (+0.015 to +0.016 AUPR; +0.016, 0.012 to 0.020, in the first check), and it is ahead in all ten hub deciles in every jackknife replicate.  Alternative adjustments agree in direction (division by the hub score +0.026; a π-residual +0.020).  The control shrinks the raw lead over CLR on M3D by one third for SC truths and by three quarters at all evidence levels, where the hub-controlled lead is small (+0.004 to +0.008).  Per regulon, attention's average precision exceeds CLR's in 66% of SC regulons on EcoMAC (P = 0.002) but in only 54% on M3D (P = 0.11), so we do not claim that attention improves most regulons.  The stratifier is derived from the attention walk; a method-neutral stratifier, such as CLR degree, is a planned sensitivity check [PENDING].

### What iterated attention trades:  resolution for aggregation

The results above have a common mechanism.  Each step of the walk replaces a gene's own pairwise dependences with those of its attended neighbourhood.  That aggregation helps where the truth is a large, loose set of genes connected through many moderate dependences, and it costs resolution where the truth is a small set of genes that are directly and strongly co-expressed, or a single direct edge.

*Direct edges.*  The benchmark of the original CLR study asks whether gene X is a direct target of transcription factor Y, scored using Y's own mRNA as a proxy for its activity (199 transcription factors, 3,971 positives, positive rate 0.48%).  On this task attention is last of the top three:  TF-node AUPR is 6.75 times random for CLR, 6.54 for GENIE3 and 5.17 for attention on M3D, and the nested bootstrap confirms the gap to CLR (−1.71 times random; −2.16 to −1.27).  GENIE3 also has the best median per-TF AUROC (EcoMAC 0.690 against 0.596 for attention) and the best median per-regulon coherence.  Our claim is therefore better recovery of regulatory programs, not better network inference.

*Large, loose regulons.*  As above, attention's per-regulon win rate is highest in regulons of 100 or more genes, and the pooled lead is carried by global regulators.

*Tight cliques.*  In K562, attention loses to CLR, GENIE3, PIDC and |r| on CORUM complexes (pooled 3.51 against 6.32–7.00 times the base rate; Table 2).  The loss is concentrated:  86% of it comes from ten ribosome and pre-rRNA complexes, whose members are among the most tightly co-expressed genes in the cell (60S ribosomal subunit average precision 0.19 for attention against 0.66 for CLR).  Per complex, CLR (+0.005), GENIE3 (+0.004) and PIDC (+0.007) beat attention; in the first run the CLR and GENIE3 differences were fragile under the delete-50% gene jackknife, and in the rerun no CORUM comparison is flagged.  On knockdown-response modules the raw tie in Table 2 is explained by hubness (the hub score alone reaches 2.42), and attention loses under hub control and per module (mean per-module AP higher than attention's by +0.097 for CLR, +0.084 for GENIE3, +0.091 for |r| and +0.100 for PIDC; attention wins 2% of modules against CLR).  This loss is not a small-module effect, since it grows with module size; it reflects tight co-expression cliques.  Two caveats apply:  62% of these modules are near-duplicates of one another, and they are built from a held-out half of the same knockdown signal, which favours direct similarity (Supplementary Note 11).

### Depth sets reach, not a discrete resolution

The trade-off suggests that depth could act as a resolution parameter, with operons best at small t and regulons and sigmulons at larger t.  We tested this and report it as a negative (Supplementary Note 12).  In a granularity sweep only operons show a sharp interior optimum (AUPR 0.30 rising to 0.50 at t = 4–5 and falling beyond t ≈ 8).  Coarser truths are flat (AUPR changes of at most 0.02 over t = 8–32) with optima at the edge of the grid, and there is no fine-to-coarse ordering of optimal depths, in *E. coli* or in K562 (P = 0.12); single large sets move the optima (dropping σS moves the sigmulon optimum from 24 to 13).  No depth helps CORUM.  A label-free multiscale analysis finds no plateaus in the number of effective modules as t grows:  it decays as a near power law, far more slowly than for a permuted operator, so the slow structure is real but continuous rather than organized in discrete levels.

What does hold is a relation between depth and size.  Per set, the best depth rises with set size for regulons (Spearman 0.34; t ∝ n^0.43) and even more for label-free correlation modules (0.44), but not for random sets (0.12) or operons.  Size, not regulatory type, predicts the optimum.  This is a generic property of diffusion, whose covering time grows with module size, and it is a mechanism that any multiscale method must respect rather than a biological finding.  The extra preference of the largest regulons for depth appears to reflect convergence toward hubs, which must be re-tested with hub-stratified scoring [PENDING].  A re-test on a logarithmic depth grid to t = 128, with control sets and per-set intervals, is planned [PENDING].

Attention width is the other resolution axis.  Data-driven choices of width failed review before they were run (one global temperature, per-gene widths from local false-discovery rates, width chosen on held-out data), and all of them, a width sweep (keff 3, 5, 10, 25) and a multi-head operator that averages walks at keff 3, 10 and 30, are being tested under a preregistration that fixes the endpoints, the Holm family and the decision rule before any truth is scored (Methods).  Held-out selections for M3D and EcoMAC have been committed; truths are not yet scored [PENDING] (Supplementary Note 15).  The multi-head operator is the preregistered candidate for recovering tight complexes without losing regulons.

### On single-cell data, context normalization transfers and the dependence measure must change

We tested transfer on the BEELINE benchmark (Pratapa et al. 2020), which comprises seven single-cell RNA-sequencing datasets, two standard gene sets and ground truths from ChIP-seq, STRING and loss- and gain-of-function screens, to which we added evidence-filtered STRING subsets.  Among the correlation- and mutual-information-based methods we evaluated, CLR is the strongest, with a median AUPRC ratio of 1.58 over 72 combinations of dataset, gene set and truth, against 1.32 for correlation, 1.20 for mutual information and 1.08 for regularized partial correlation (Fig. 7).  The held-out depth transfers, falling at t* = 5–11 in all 14 dataset and gene-set cases and bracketing the bacterial t*, although depth was never tuned on single-cell data.

Mutual information is, however, a poor dependence measure for zero-inflated counts, because the B-spline estimator spends its resolution on the point mass at zero.  Replacing it by the absolute correlation of normal scores (Blom scores, with tied zeros sharing one score; CLR-NS) improves CLR in every gene context we tried (median AUPRC ratio +0.07 to +0.12; Supplementary Note 2).  Attention on these scores adds nothing over CLR-NS, and wider gene contexts (5,000 most variable genes, all expressed genes, driven genes) give no gain either.

We then ran GENIE3, GRNBoost2 and PIDC on the same 14 cases and compared methods with tests blocked by dataset (n = 7; Supplementary Note 13).  On BEELINE's primary truths CLR, which was fixed in advance, is comparable to GENIE3 and PIDC (mean log2 AUPRC ratio −0.04 and −0.02 relative to CLR; each better than CLR in 3 and 4 of 7 datasets; P = 0.69 and 0.81) and our GRNBoost2 runs score below it in 7 of 7 datasets (−0.35; P = 0.016; see below).  CLR-NS has the highest mean (GENIE3 −0.10 and PIDC −0.08 relative to it, each better in 1 of 7 datasets; P = 0.11 and 0.16), but it was chosen on these truths, so this comparison is not confirmatory.  The two human datasets split:  on hESC, GENIE3 and PIDC beat CLR-NS (driven by STRING), and on hHep CLR-NS beats all three.  Cell-type-specific ChIP-seq truths are near random for every method (median AUPRC ratio 0.98–1.02), as BEELINE reported.  GRNBoost2 ranks last everywhere in our hands, bulk and single cell, whereas BEELINE placed it near GENIE3; we are checking our implementation before making any claim that involves it [PENDING].

## Discussion

CLR anticipated the central move of attention:  normalize each query's compatibilities within its own context, so that weights mean the same thing across queries on very different scales.  Our results suggest that this move, more than the choice of compatibility function, is what carries over.  Once the normalization is in place, the rest of the attention machinery adds measurable value, namely a temperature that fixes how many genes each gene attends to, aggregation of multi-step attention mass and a depth chosen without labels.  Without the normalization, the same machinery is ordinary network propagation and is markedly weaker, and replacing the softmax by hard, FDR-selected graphs is also weaker (soft attention beats the best hard-FDR operator in all 200 paired draws).  The dependence measure becomes important where the data demand it:  mutual information where batch structure bends relationships, and rank-based correlation where counts are zero-inflated.

What the completed operator adds is reach, and reach has a price.  Iterated attention ranks pairs within large, loose regulatory programs better than single-step scores do, which is why it leads pooled recovery on *E. coli* even after hub control.  It does not rank pairs within each regulon better than CLR or GENIE3, it dilutes the sharpest direct dependences, and it blurs tight cliques such as ribosomal complexes.  In practice, single-step CLR or GENIE3 should be used to nominate direct edges and complexes, and attention to rank large regulatory programs and target sets.  Depth controls how far the aggregation reaches, but it is not a dial with discrete settings:  the slow structure of the walk is continuous, and the optimal depth scales with module size as diffusion predicts.

Estimated attention differs from learned attention in two respects.  Its scores carry statistical semantics:  each CLR score is a standardized deviation from a gene's own background with a permutation null, so the scores, though not the iterated attention mass, can be thresholded at a controlled false-discovery rate and audited edge by edge.  And nothing is learned:  no corpus, embeddings or labels are needed, which matters most where elements have no fixed meaning, as genes do not across tissues and conditions.  Whether this makes it a yardstick, a complement or a component for learned attention is the question of the preregistered comparison [PENDING].

The gains are modest in absolute terms, about 0.02 AUPR or 16% relative on M3D, but consistent across resampling schemes, including a nested bootstrap that re-selects depth in every draw, and they survive control for global expression programs and for hub genes on strong-evidence regulons.  In human K562 cells they are small and confined to high-confidence transcription-factor targets, and on single-cell data attention adds nothing to rank-based CLR.  An extension of the same machinery to perturbation targets, in which genes are scored by how much a perturbation's experiments change their attention rows, is promising but not established and is the subject of separate work (Supplementary Note 6).

Several limitations apply.  RegulonDB evidence derives in part from expression studies; the strong/confirmed tier limits this, and any bias applies equally to every method compared.  The depth criterion predicts held-out CLR structure, a proxy for biological accuracy rather than the thing itself, and the transferred depth was chosen on M3D.  The method comparison's intervals resample genes with networks held fixed, and the hub stratifier is derived from the attention walk.  EcoMAC may share arrays with M3D, and its core result does not yet have held-out depth and experiment-level intervals [PENDING], so the main bacterial results rest on one organism and, until that audit, on what may not be two independent compendia.  The human results come from one compendium, the K562 essential-gene screen; genome-wide K562 and RPE1 compendia are being analysed [PENDING].

## Methods

**Data.**  The *E. coli* analyses use M3D *E. coli* v4 build 6 (4,297 genes), with the 466 replicate-averaged experiments as the primary set and the 907 arrays for the replicate-aware bootstraps, and EcoMAC (Carrera et al. 2014; 2,198 arrays, 4,189 genes), with each array's Affymetrix platform taken from its GEO record and ComBat correction by platform in the method comparison.  The ground truth is RegulonDB 14.5 (RISet, TUSet, OperonSet and NetworkSigmaGene), with 2008-era gene names mapped to b-numbers through NCBI synonyms.  The human compendium is the K562 essential-gene Perturb-seq screen (Replogle et al. 2022):  each sample is one knockdown's normalized pseudobulk expression change against control cells, for knockdowns with a significant transcriptional phenotype (energy test), split at random into halves A and B.  Networks are built on half A; the literature truths are DoRothEA A–B and A–C, CollecTRI and CORUM complexes (via OmniPath), and knockdown-response modules are built from half B only, so they share no samples with the networks.  Gene symbols duplicated in the data are dropped.  Single-cell data and networks are from BEELINE (Zenodo 3701939); graded binding is from ChIP-Atlas, and the evidence-filtered STRING truths use STRING v11.0 transcriptional actions and the experimental and database channels of v12.0.

**Dependence estimation.**  Mutual information uses the B-spline estimator of Daub et al. (2004) with spline order 3 and one common bin count chosen for the joint histogram by the low-bias rule of Hacine-Gharbi et al. (2012); per-gene adaptive bins inflate the mutual information of heavy-tailed genes.  Normal scores use the Blom transform, Φ⁻¹((r − 3/8)/(n + 1/4)), with average ranks for ties.

**Context normalization.**  Each row of the dependence matrix is z-scored against the gene's own background (sample standard deviation, negative values clipped to zero), and the two directions are combined by Stouffer's rule, (z_ij + z_ji)/√2, which has a standard-normal null; the original Euclidean rule gives nearly identical rankings (Spearman 0.993).  For edge selection, samples are permuted independently for each gene, CLR is recomputed and Benjamini–Hochberg selection is applied at q = 0.05; the selected edge sets are unchanged between 100 and 1,000 permutations.

**Attention operator.**  For each row, the candidates are the 50 highest positive scores and the weights are a softmax whose temperature is found by bisection so that exp(entropy) = 10.  Rows sum to one, rows without candidates keep a self-loop, and P = (1 − α)I + αA with α = 0.5 is read out as ½(Pᵗ + Pᵗᵀ).  Caps of 100 and 200 candidates give the same depth and gain as 50; an uncapped softmax selects shallower depths and gains less AUPR (Supplementary Note 4).

**Held-out depth.**  For bulk data, experiments are split into two balanced halves by laboratory (experimenter) and each half is used in turn for training.  The criterion is the AUPR of the training-half attention mass for the top 1% of the held-out half's scores computed from the same dependence measure, and t* is the geometric mean of the two directions' optima.  For the K562 compendium and single-cell data, knockdowns or cells are split into random halves.  RegulonDB, human and BEELINE truths are never used to choose t.  The method comparison uses t = 9, the median over 50 M3D splits, on every dataset.

**Competitor methods.**  GENIE3 (random forests, 1,000 trees; GENIE3 R package), GRNBoost2 (arboreto) and PIDC (NetworkInference.jl) are run with every gene as a candidate regulator; directed weights are symmetrized as max(w_ij, w_ji), and the resulting gene × gene matrices enter every benchmark in the same run as |r|, CLR and attention.  PIDC's cost grows with the cube of the gene count, so on K562 every method is run on the 5,671 genes that appear in at least one literature truth.  On human single-cell data PIDC falls back from a gamma to a normal approximation for under 1% of pairs, including pairs involving SOX3 and PAX6.  GRNBoost2 returns most pairs tied at zero (87–93% on bulk data); an audit of our implementation against BEELINE's own output is running [PENDING].

**Benchmarks.**  Co-membership AUPR pools all gene pairs among annotated genes, excludes same-operon pairs and is reported as such or divided by the base rate.  Coherence is the AUROC, per regulon, of within-regulon pairs against pairs of a member and a non-member; we report its median over regulons.  Per-regulon average precision (AP) is computed over the same candidates as coherence, with ties broken in expectation, for regulons with at least 10 positive non-operon pairs; a gene in several regulons counts in each, and regulons are not merged.  The TF-node benchmark scores directed TF → target pairs for 199 RegulonDB transcription factors (pooled AUPR over random, median per-TF AUROC).  BEELINE cases are scored by the directed TF-node AUPRC ratio and early precision ratio, separately for BEELINE's primary truths and our derived truths.

**Statistics.**  All intervals are 95% and all tests two-sided unless stated.

- *Paired delete-half gene jackknife (pooled AUPR).*  In each of 100 replicates, half of the annotated genes are drawn without replacement and every method is scored on the pairs whose two genes are both kept.  The standard error of a method's AUPR, or of the difference between two methods computed on the same replicates, is the standard deviation over replicates (for delete-d jackknife with d = n/2 the variance factor (n − d)/d is 1), and intervals are the estimate ± 1.96 SE.  The networks are held fixed, so these intervals reflect uncertainty over genes, not over experiments, cells or random seeds.  In Table 2, ties are called when the Bonferroni-corrected interval of the paired difference from the best method in the row includes 0.
- *Regulon bootstrap (coherence).*  Regulons are resampled with replacement (1,000 draws) and the median of the paired per-regulon differences is recomputed; intervals are percentiles.
- *Cluster and replicate bootstraps over experiments.*  In the cluster bootstrap the 466 experiments are drawn with replacement and, for each occurrence, one of that experiment's replicate arrays is drawn from the 907; dependences, CLR scores, attention and every metric are recomputed in each of 1,000 draws, and intervals are 2.5–97.5% percentiles.  The replicate bootstrap (200 draws) keeps every experiment once and draws one array per experiment.  In the nested bootstrap each cluster draw also splits its own laboratories at random and re-selects t before scoring.  Hard FDR operators were scored on 200 draws paired with draws of the main run.
- *Hub stratification.*  π is the stationary distribution of the attention walk and H_ij = π_i + π_j.  Gene pairs are assigned to ten quantile strata of H, each method's scores are converted to percentile ranks within each stratum, and pooled AUPR is computed on these percentiles, so that pairs are compared only with pairs of similar hubness.  The hub-only baseline ranks pairs by H.  Division by H and residualization on π are reported as sensitivity analyses.
- *Per-regulon (macro) AP.*  For each regulon the paired difference ΔAP = AP(comparator) − AP(attention) is computed within one run (negative favours attention); we summarize its mean and median over regulons.  Uncertainty over regulons uses a BCa bootstrap for the mean (P by inverting the interval) and the Wilcoxon signed-rank test for the median.  Uncertainty over genes uses a delete-20% gene jackknife (200 replicates; variance factor (n − d)/d = 4), with the regulon set fixed to regulons keeping at least three positive pairs in every replicate; a delete-50% jackknife is a stability check, flagged when the 95% interval of the ratio of standard errors lies outside ±25%.  A difference counts only if both intervals exclude 0, and Holm correction is applied to the larger of the two P values.  Because the jackknife keeps only regulons scored in every replicate, which drops small regulons, the two intervals of this rule cover different regulon sets.
- *Multiplicity.*  Holm families were fixed before scoring, with missing tests counted in the family size:  per-regulon AP on *E. coli*, 5 comparators × 2 compendia × 2 evidence levels × mean and median = 40 tests; on K562 (primary, truth-gene run), 5 comparators × 5 truths × 2 = 50 tests; K562 secondary (all genes, not independent of the primary) 40 tests.  The width preregistration uses one Holm family of 32 tests.  The learned-attention preregistration has a single primary contrast and a Holm family of two key secondary tests.  The comparisons of hard FDR operators (about 12) are reported with marginal intervals.
- *Blocked tests on BEELINE.*  Per dataset, log2 AUPRC ratios are averaged over gene sets and truths, and methods are compared with exact Wilcoxon signed-rank and sign tests over the seven datasets (smallest attainable two-sided P = 0.0156).  P values over the 72 dataset × gene set × truth combinations are not treated as independent evidence.

**Preregistered analyses.**  The width test (`docs/preregistration_keff.md`, fixed 2026-10-16, changes logged 2026-10-21) compares keff 3, 5 and 25, a multi-head operator (keff 3, 10, 30), one global temperature, per-gene widths from local false-discovery rates and two held-out width selections against keff = 10, each at its own held-out depth.  The primary endpoint is the difference in pooled AUPR on M3D and EcoMAC transcription-factor regulons and on K562 CollecTRI and CORUM, with a smallest effect of interest of ±0.005 and Holm over 32 tests; the headline changes only if a variant wins on at least three of four endpoints, loses on none and replicates on both bacterial compendia.  Held-out selections are committed before the truth phase, which refuses to run unless they match; smoke tests on 300-gene subsets that touched truths are disclosed in the change log.  The learned-attention comparison (`docs/preregistration_scfm.md`, fixed 2026-10-21) has one primary contrast, CLR-NS against scPRINT zero-shot attention, tested for non-inferiority with a margin of 0.15 log2 units through the exact Wilcoxon (Hodges–Lehmann) interval over the seven BEELINE datasets; CLR-NS was chosen on these truths, which the preregistration discloses, and CLR serves as a key secondary reference.

**Software.**  The R package `clr` has a C++17 core with OpenMP and a thin Rcpp layer; analyses are scripted in `analysis/` and run through a capped job queue.  Code is available at [repository].

Statistical designs and results were additionally checked by independent instances of the same model acting as reviewers before being accepted; the preregistrations were drafted in this way and fixed before scoring.  All analysis code, logs and preregistrations are available in the repository.  The authors reviewed, verified and take full responsibility for all code, results and text.
